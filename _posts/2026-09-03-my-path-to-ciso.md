## Starting with a question nobody could answer for me

I didn't set out to become a CISO. I set out to understand how things
break. Somewhere between my BSc in Computer Engineering and
the first time I got a shell on a box I wasn't supposed to have access
to, the question changed shape. It stopped being "how does this work"
and became "how does this fail, and who's responsible when it does."
That second question is the one that's been quietly steering everything
since.

I think a lot of people who end up chasing security leadership start the
same way I did: technical, curious, a little obsessed with the puzzle of
it. Nobody wakes up one day and decides "I want to manage risk for a
living." You get pulled into it, one broken thing at a time, until you
realize the person who understands why something broke is the person
everyone eventually turns to.

## Building the technical floor first

I've always believed you can't lead security If you don't understand security in the first place.
So, I've spent most of my energy so far building the technical floor under
me before I begin think about the ceiling. That has meant taking certifications; 
CompTIA Network+, Security+, PenTest+, and CNVP, not as
trophies, but as forced checkpoints. Each one made me sit with material
I would have otherwise skimmed past.

It has also meant hours spent in HackTheBox labs, at first alone but later as part
of the HackTheBox Cameroon local community and the global Hackthebox community; where machines stopped being
abstract puzzles and started being conversations. Other people's
approaches, other people's dead ends, other people's "wait, why didn't I
think of that." Community indeed changes how fast you learn. It's one thing to
grind a box by yourself for six hours. It's another to watch someone
else solve it in twenty minutes and realize your mental model of the
attack surface was just wrong.

OWASP Cameroon and the OWASP global community gave me the other half of that: the defensive lens next
to the offensive one. It's easy, when you're deep in exploit development
or privilege escalation chains, to forget that the whole point is
prevention. Sitting in rooms with people thinking about secure
architecture and threat modeling has been a useful correction every
time I start to enjoy breaking things a little too much for their own
sake.

## Why the offensive path, specifically

I could have aimed for a straighter administrative route into security
leadership; more governance, more compliance, less time in a terminal.
I chose offensive security instead, deliberately. My reasoning is
simple: the best security leaders I've read about and studied all seem
to share one thing. They've been on the attacking side long enough to
have real, earned intuition about what a determined adversary actually
does, not just what a framework says they might do.

A CISO who has never tried to break-in is making decisions from theory.
A CISO who has spent years finding the gaps, chaining small
misconfigurations into full compromises, and watching how real attackers
think, is making decisions from experience. I want to be the second
kind.

## What's still ahead

I'm currently working through my MSc in Network Engineering and Security,
with my thesis focused on honeynet-based threat intelligence within Zero
Trust Architecture, which feels like a natural continuation of this
whole arc. Attack, understand, defend, repeat, but now at the level of
architecture and strategy instead of a single machine.

I don't think there's a shortcut to CISO. What I think is that; there's a long
accumulation of technical depth, community, teaching, and enough
failures to build real judgment, and then, eventually, someone trusts
you with the responsibility of protecting something bigger than
yourself. I'm not there yet, but every CTF, every writeup, every
certification and every effort is a deliberate brick in that direction.

If you're earlier on this same path, still building the technical
floor, still grinding boxes, still not sure the "leadership" part will
ever be relevant to you, I'd say: it will. Just not yet. Keep building.
